How Should a Property Manager Handle a No Hot Water Call? My AI Took Two, Including a Gas Smell

Ping Hsu, Founder of Acel Cortex. Runs an 800-unit property management company.
Published Nov 24, 2026
Short answer

Start by confirming who the tenant is and which unit, then narrow the problem down. Is it every tap or one, was a lot of hot water just used, and is the heater gas or electric with a flashing light? If it is simply used up, the tenant waits 45 to 60 minutes and tries again. If the heater shows a fault, a technician goes out. And if the tenant mentions a gas smell at any point, safety comes first: they leave the unit, call 911 or the gas company from outside, and your on-call person is alerted right away.

"I don't have any hot water" is one of the most common calls a property manager gets, and one of the most vague. It could be a tenant who just ran three loads of laundry. It could be a tripped breaker, an unplugged heater or a gas problem. You cannot send a technician for every one of those, and you cannot ignore any of them either.

So I called my own AI maintenance line as a tenant with no hot water. Then I called back and said I smelled gas. This post walks through both calls. If you want the bigger picture of how the phone line works, approvals and all, start with how our AI answers tenant maintenance calls.

Four things have to be right before anything else

Before the AI can solve a problem over the phone or send the right vendor, it has to get four things right on every call:

  1. Confirm it is your tenant.
  2. Confirm the property and the unit. On a portal request you already know this. On a phone call or text you do not.
  3. Diagnose the problem with a few safe questions.
  4. Decide if it is urgent or an emergency.

If any of these is shaky, you cannot trust what happens next. That is why these four steps come before any troubleshooting.

Call 1: no hot water

Here is how the call went, step by step.

It made sure it had the right person and unit

The agent guessed the wrong name at first, because someone on my team had just tested the line from the same phone. I corrected it. When the address I gave did not quite match what was on file, it asked me to double-check the unit number before going any further. That is exactly what I want. An AI that skips this step books the right repair at the wrong address.

It narrowed the problem down

Then it worked through the same questions my team would ask:

  • Is it every tap, or just one tap or the shower? Every tap points to the heater, not a fixture.
  • Has a lot of hot water been used in the last hour, like laundry or a bath? If so, the tenant can wait 45 to 60 minutes and try again. I said I did laundry three hours ago, and it correctly ruled that out as the cause.
  • Is the heater gas or electric?
  • Is there a light or display on the heater, and is it flashing or showing a code? It also told me not to try relighting it myself.
  • Is there any water around the base? No leak.

It knew when to hand off

A flashing light on a gas heater, with laundry ruled out, means a person needs to look at it. The agent told me one of our team would follow up, confirmed the best number to call me back on, and alerted our on-duty person. In this case the fix is simple: we send a technician to reset the heater.

The value here is in the calls that never reach my team. No hot water can mean a lot of things: electrical, gas, an unplugged heater or a tripped breaker. Our diagnosis guide covers each one in the same order we would check them. If the tenant had just used up the hot water, the AI would have told them to wait an hour and call back if it was still cold, and nobody on my team would have touched it.

If your office gets ten maintenance calls a day and six or seven can be sorted out this way, you only deal with the two or three that need you. Your day stops getting interrupted by questions that answer themselves.

Call 2: a gas smell

Then I called back and said I thought I could smell gas. This is where a maintenance line has to behave completely differently, and it did.

Safety first, before any details

The agent did not ask for my name. The first thing it said was to leave the unit now, leave the door open and not flip any light switches or use anything that could spark. Once outside, it told me to call 911 or the local gas company, and gave me the gas company's number for our area. Then it asked me to tell it once I was outside and safe.

It did not let me talk it down

I pushed back. I said it was probably minor, my windows were open, and I just wanted a technician sent over. The agent held its ground: a gas smell is treated as urgent even if it seems small. Leave now, and call from outside.

That is the right answer. Tenants downplay things all the time, and the cost of being wrong about gas is too high to negotiate.

Then it took the details

Only after I said I was outside did it move on. It told me getting out was the right call, took my name and address, confirmed the callback number and said our on-duty person had been alerted. It told me to stay outside until the gas company or 911 said it was safe to go back in. When I asked when someone would call, it was honest that it could not give an exact time.

It also got my name wrong on this call. I did not bother correcting it. A small miss, but worth knowing about.

What my team saw while I was still on the phone

By the time I hung up, the work had already happened:

  • The on-call person got a text right away, as soon as the AI decided it was an emergency. It said the caller smelled gas, was now outside and safe, and asked them to call back.
  • The tenant got a text confirming the request was received and the maintenance team would keep them updated.
  • A work order opened in our property management software, with the vendor notified.
  • The full call was saved, with the recording available to replay.

This is the same playbook my team follows. The difference is that the AI runs it the same way every time, at any hour, and nothing depends on someone remembering a step.

FAQ

Should a tenant relight a gas water heater pilot themselves?

No. Our AI tells tenants not to try relighting the heater. A flashing light or error code on a gas heater goes to a technician, and if there is any gas smell at all, the tenant leaves the unit first.

How long does it take hot water to come back after laundry or a bath?

Usually 45 to 60 minutes. That is the wait our AI gives a tenant who just used a lot of hot water, with the instruction to call back if it is still cold after that.

Who should a tenant call if they smell gas?

911 or their local gas utility, from outside the unit. Our AI gives the tenant the gas company's emergency number for their area, then alerts our on-call person once the tenant is safe.

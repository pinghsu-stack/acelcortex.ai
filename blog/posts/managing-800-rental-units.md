Some days I genuinely hate this business. We manage over 800 units, and some days I am tired, drained and ready to shut it all down. Here are the worst parts of growing from 20 to 800 units, what fixed each one, and why I am still here.

## 1. Nobody sees the work you do

When things go right, nobody notices. When something breaks, the owner asks why you picked that vendor, and the tenant says you are being cheap at the expense of their family.

Early on, owners asked me, "Why am I paying you a management fee?" after one thing went wrong, ignoring everything that went right. At $150 to $300 a month, that fee does not buy a lot of anyone's time.

**The fix: show your work.** We now spell out what we did on the owner statement. It changes the conversation from "Why am I paying for this?" to "Here is everything that was done, and here is what comes next."

## 2. You get blamed for things you cannot control

A main drain clogs at 8 p.m. and water backs up. Tenants upstairs keep running water. The vendor who promised to come right away shows up the next morning. The floor is damaged, the owner blames you, and the tenants blow up your phone. You are in the middle, absorbing everyone's frustration.

**The fix: a deeper vendor bench.** We now keep several vendors in every category, so when one falls through, we are not stuck.

## 3. You chase money that is not yours

Chasing rent from five tenants is easy. At 100 or 200 units, you will have tenants who push boundaries, and some professional tenants who do it on purpose. Meanwhile the owner says if you do not collect, they cannot afford your fee.

That bothered me. We do all the heavy lifting and earn 8 to 10 percent. A collection agency keeps 25 to 50 percent of what it recovers. In a tenant-friendly market like ours, the process takes months: 14 days of notice, more filing time, two to three months for a hearing, and sometimes 10 to 12 months in total. One professional tenant looked at us and asked, "What are you going to do?"

**The fix: a base fee.** We now charge a base fee whether rent comes in or not, because the work happens either way.

## 4. You are never off

The phone rings on weekends. Tenants even call when two neighbors are arguing. If you are the breaking point of your business and have nothing left for your family, you cannot grow it.

**The fix: rotation and clear emergency rules.** Our team takes turns on call, and anything that is not an emergency waits until business hours. Teaching tenants and owners that some things can wait takes repetition, but it sticks.

## 5. More doors means more problems, not more margin

Onboarding more properties means onboarding more problems. That means more property managers, more technicians and more payroll, and more people whose feelings and relationships you have to manage. Many owners hire to free up time, then step back in every time someone burns out.

**The fix: offshore and AI.** We moved much of the work to a virtual assistant team, and now AI handles what should not be done by people at all: data transfer, billing, and the first diagnosis of tenant requests. That buffer is what keeps our team from burning out.

## Why I am still in this business

Looking back, most of what I complained about was not property management itself. It was repetitive work, and repetitive work can be solved.

What nobody told me is that **passive income is the finish line, not the starting point**. Once your systems can handle your current size, you manage the system instead of every relationship, and you can step back.

And if you quit, you lose the good with the bad: off-market deals, relationships with investors, and a team that can manage your own rentals. Without it, I would be back in a 9-to-5.

## FAQ

### Is property management a stressful business?

Yes, especially as you grow. You sit between owners, tenants and vendors, and you are blamed when anything goes wrong. The stress drops a lot once you have systems: clear owner reporting, backup vendors, emergency-only after-hours rules and automation for repetitive work.

### Should property managers charge a fee when rent is not collected?

We do. The work of chasing rent, serving notices and dealing with the tenant happens whether or not rent comes in, so a base fee makes sure that work is paid.

### How do I stop tenants from calling me after hours?

Define what counts as an emergency, put it in writing for tenants and owners, and route calls through a phone line with business hours. Then hold the line, because it takes repetition before everyone learns that non-urgent issues wait.

Have you ever felt owners are harder to deal with than tenants? An owner calls at 8 or 9 p.m. saying they made an arrangement for tomorrow and need you there on short notice. It feels like your time is not respected.

These are the seven rules we enforce at our property management company so it stops happening.

## 1. Set office hours for owners

Owners do not have access to you after hours, say 7 p.m. You need some flexibility, but you are already on standby for emergency repairs. You should not be on standby for everything else too.

Most owner requests, like statement questions, charges and coordination, can wait until morning. Set it up so those messages land in your inbox for review the next day. Make the rule clear from day one and write it down so you can point to it.

## 2. Be the tenant's only point of contact

When I started, I tried to be accommodating, so tenants talked to both me and the owner. Whenever I said no to a request, like an upgrade, painting or an extra occupant, the tenant went around me to the owner. The owner, wanting the problem gone, often said yes. After that, no rule could be enforced.

Do not co-manage with owners. Tenants talk only to you, and any request needs the owner's approval through you. That buffer is what owners pay you for. If tenants keep reaching them, they will eventually ask what they are paying for. If an owner insists on staying in direct contact with tenants, cut it off early, or it will backfire within a few months.

## 3. Define emergencies, and fix them without waiting

If you wait for owner approval during an emergency, a busy or cash-strapped owner may pick and choose what to fix. You carry liability for the properties you manage, so that is not acceptable.

Define emergencies up front: heat, water and safety issues like break-ins and lockouts. Those get fixed, no negotiation. Also agree on the dollar threshold where you need approval.

Example: a roof starts leaking. That is water damage, so we patch it right away to stop the leak, with no approval needed. The full roof replacement can wait, so we get two or three quotes and the owner approves one.

## 4. You choose the vendors

Owners often have their own vendors. If they have a close relationship with one, we will look at them and may add them to our list. But we do not pick vendors together with the owner every time. Owners who are not dealing with tenants respond slowly, and we are the ones who take the heat when a job drags.

Our volume with local vendors gets us more reliable people and better prices. "I have a guy who can do it cheaper" does not mean better. If an owner's vendor cannot meet our schedule or process, we use ours. You were hired to do the job, so do it.

## 5. Hold a property reserve, and do not become a 0% lender

Owners will ask you to cover a bill and take it out of next month's rent. That makes you their interest-free lender.

Two fixes:

- **A property reserve** held in the trust account, for example $500 for a single-family home and $750 to $1,000 for a multifamily property. It covers repairs and emergencies, and gets topped up from rent. It is for maintenance only.
- **Fees for extra tasks.** Paying property tax, utilities or insurance is asset management, not property management. If owners want you to handle it, charge a fee for the coordination.

## 6. Put listing the property in your management agreement

Owners who struggle to manage their property often decide to sell it later. If another agent lists it who knows nothing about the property, its history or the tenants, your job gets much harder.

Make listing the property for sale part of your management agreement. Owners mostly care about getting the right price, and you know the property best. Make it standard and you will get the listing most of the time. If an owner insists on their own agent, be flexible, but raise your management fee to cover the extra work. In 2019, making this standard for our management clients netted us about $200,000.

## 7. Ask owners to judge you on the process

Owners who interfere usually feel they are losing control. Give them a fair way to hold you accountable: judge us on the process we agreed to. If we do not do what we said, call it out. But the process applies to both sides.

Review your management agreement every 6 to 12 months to keep simplifying the operation.

## FAQ

### What counts as an emergency repair in property management?

Anything involving heat, water or safety, like a burst pipe, no heat, a break-in or a lockout. Write your list into the management agreement so owners agree upfront that these get fixed without waiting for approval.

### How much should a property reserve be?

We hold about $500 for a single-family home and $750 to $1,000 for a multifamily property, kept in the trust account and topped up from rent. Use it only for maintenance.

### Can a property manager stop owners from talking to tenants?

You cannot stop an owner, but you can make it a condition of management. Explain that tenants going around you undoes every rule you set, and that the buffer is part of what they pay you for.

Most property management companies do not have departments. They have one or two property managers wearing every hat, and a few spreadsheets holding it all together. That is not a business. That is a job.

I used to talk to owners and tenants all day, then process invoices at night, until I decided I needed a real structure. That structure changed everything, including our margin.

## What a property manager actually does

Follow a vacant unit from start to finish:

- Market it, list it and run showings.
- Screen tenants with credit and background checks.
- Inspect the unit, set up the tenant's ledger and collect rent.
- Handle communication with tenants, vendors and owners.
- Handle repairs and preventive maintenance.

That breaks into three jobs, and a property management company is those three jobs done in a structured way.

## The three departments

1. **Leasing:** marketing, showings, screening and new leases.
2. **Accounting and admin:** rent collection, bookkeeping, owner approvals and daily communication.
3. **Maintenance:** repairs, preventive maintenance, technicians and vendors.

Start with a direction, like 500 units in five years. Then draw the org chart that would support it: you as owner and CEO, a manager and agents in leasing, an office manager and bookkeeper in accounting and admin, and a manager with technicians and vendors in maintenance. You will not fill it on day one, but keep the full chart in mind so every hire fits it.

## Who to put in first

Fill the seats that make money before the seats that manage.

- **You start as the leasing team.** Leasing has the highest margin, about one month's rent per lease. A few leases pay your salary right away, and in a market where rents are $2,000 to $4,000, four leases can fund your next hire.
- **A part-time bookkeeper next.** You need someone tracking the numbers early. Remote and part time is fine.
- **Then technicians.** As you pass 30 to 50 units, maintenance coordination takes over your time. Technicians, or vendors at first, earn a margin on every job.
- **Then managers.** Only when you cannot keep up with your agents and technicians do you add a leasing manager or maintenance manager. A good maintenance manager does more than coordinate: they spot extra work worth suggesting to owners while technicians are on site.

## The mistake most owners make

Most owners keep themselves in the management role and hire people to take management work off their plate, like an assistant property manager, without adding anyone who brings in revenue. That only adds overhead.

It also hurts quality. If one manager does three jobs, and you multiply that across the company as you grow, the work gets inconsistent fast.

## Where AI fits in the structure

The coordination between departments creates a lot of paperwork. We used to hand it to virtual assistants. Now AI agents:

- Put together rental applications, build the report and assess fit against each unit's criteria for the leasing team.
- Process vendor invoices using our standard pricing and procedures.
- Follow up on unpaid rent.

So across our 800 units, the core team is only a few employees. Leasing agents and technicians are independent contractors, and AI handles the admin in between. Keeping the departmental structure this tight is how **our margin went from 14 percent to 32 percent**.

## FAQ

### What departments does a property management company need?

Three: leasing, accounting and admin, and maintenance. Even if you are the only person in the company, think of your work in those three buckets so each one can be handed off cleanly later.

### What is a good profit margin for a property management company?

Ours went from 14 percent to 32 percent after we moved to a departmental structure and automated the admin work. The biggest lever was hiring for revenue-generating roles instead of more management.

### Should my leasing agents and technicians be employees or contractors?

We use independent contractors for leasing agents and vendors, and keep the core team small. It keeps fixed costs low while volume grows. Check your local employment rules before deciding.

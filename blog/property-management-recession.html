Is Property Management Recession-Proof? It Kept Me Afloat When I Lost Around $1 Million

Ping Hsu, Founder of Acel Cortex. Runs an 800-unit property management company.
Published Nov 27, 2026
Short answer

Not fully, but it is one of the steadiest businesses in real estate. When rates spiked in 2022, I lost around $1 million across flips, sales and other projects, while my property management company kept paying my bills every month. Management fees come in whether deals close or not. The five lessons: protect your core business, put cash flow ahead of fast money, cut overhead before you are forced to, systemize with AI, and do not let good debt turn into bad debt.

Back in 2021, a lot of real estate people looked like geniuses, me included. Whatever we bought made money. Then the market turned, and it put a lot of them out of business. It almost put me out too.

I lost around $1 million, but not in my property management company. I lost it in everything I had built around it. The one business that kept paying my bills the whole time was the boring one: property management.

What I had going in 2021

In 2021 I had a lot going on:

  • 11 to 12 flip projects running at the same time.
  • 9 agents on my sales team.
  • A coaching business for investors that was really taking off.
  • The property management company, which I had mostly stopped paying attention to.

All of it was growing, and honestly, very little of that was skill. It was timing. Rates were low, the market was booming, and almost every decision made money whether it was a good decision or not.

What happened when rates spiked in 2022

When interest rates started climbing in 2022, I told myself it was just another correction. Maybe even a time to buy more.

It was not. My flip projects got stuck. Lenders and other investors were involved in them, so it was not just my money on the line. The first year, I tried to keep renovation costs as low as possible. The bigger problem was overhead. I had a big payroll and almost no transactions, because the market had frozen. Expenses stayed high, income dropped hard, and we were losing money every single month.

I did not understand how serious it was until about 6 months in. That is when the short-term loans came up for renewal and lenders wanted new appraisals. The values came in lower, so I had to put in cash to cover the shortfall just to renew, at much higher interest rates. A lot of the money in my bank account was suddenly no longer liquid. Some of the smaller investment groups we worked with went out of business because they could not hold on.

The question that changed how I see property management

While I was stabilizing everything, someone asked me how I managed to protect my personal income through all of it.

I had to stop and think. My income never came from the flips. It never came from sales commissions or from coaching. It came from property management. I was on payroll there, even though I was barely involved in the day-to-day anymore, because we had a team running it consistently.

The margins were not big, but it paid the bills. That meant almost everything I earned from anything else could go straight back into the business or into paying down debt.

When the market was flying, steady did not seem like enough. When the market froze, steady gave me room to breathe. That changed how I run everything now. Property management is the core business, and everything else feeds into it.

5 lessons from losing around $1 million

1. Protect your core business

If you have something that works and does not need much of your time, protect it and keep improving it. I spread myself across too many things and nearly starved the one business that was actually paying me.

2. Cash flow is still king, in business too

In real estate everyone knows you cannot gamble on negative cash flow. The same goes for your business. I spent years chasing fast money over steady cash flow. I made a lot of money, but it was not income that could last 5, 10 or 15 years. Management fees can.

3. Cut overhead before you are forced to

I only started cutting overhead about 6 months after the market turned, which was far too late. I should never have carried that much in the first place. Back then I thought growth was always the answer. More often, staying lean and profitable is the better decision. Only add overhead when you can see the return, and only scale once you know exactly what your winning formula is. I did not. I was shooting in the dark.

4. Build systems, and use AI to run them

There is still too much manual work in most property management companies. Using AI properly is not asking it a few questions. It is getting measurable results, saving hours per task, not minutes. Once I figured that out, I realized one person can get through the work of 5 or 10, without the overhead of hiring them. Your cost is the AI usage itself. If you turn that into more productive hours, more sales or lower overhead, you see it on your financials right away. 5. Good debt can turn into bad debt very quickly

If you do not have enough cash flow, or you are overleveraged, what everyone calls good debt becomes a huge liability. It happened to me. I had to close out 11 projects. Four years later, I am still dealing with it, and there are 2 projects I am still putting money into every month.

When the market is good and rates are low, people downplay the risk because everything runs on hype. That is not always the case. Do not refinance too quickly, do not overleverage, keep debt as low as you can and keep building your reserves. If your reserves are thin, focus on the business that brings in cash every month.

For me, that business is property management. It is hard work, but it is a boring business that brings in cash every single month.

FAQ

Can a property management company feed your other real estate businesses?

Yes, and that is the part I underestimated. A lot of our deal flow comes from landlords who want to exit. Some of the investors who end up investing with us found us through property management. Even today, over 55% of our sales come through the property management side.

Is a property management company a sellable asset?

It is. Steady management fees make it an asset you can sell down the road, and it tends to be worth more when your industry picks up. That is one more reason not to sacrifice it while you chase faster money somewhere else.

If you were starting over, would you start with property management or flipping?

Property management. If I started over, I would still begin with property management and use the other businesses to feed into it, but I would run it leaner and never stop growing it. I would also try not to grow any other part of the business too fast, because the rest grows when property management grows.
